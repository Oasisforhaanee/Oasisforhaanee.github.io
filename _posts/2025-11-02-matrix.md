Anyone who has taken university math has likely heard the word *matrix*. Matrices are such a central concept that they can be regarded as one of the mathematical foundations of linear algebra, calculus, and beyond. In this post, I’d like to talk about the meaning of matrices and why they matter, drawing on what I have been reviewing myself.

## Matrix

A matrix is, literally speaking, numbers placed into rows and columns. But the meaning behind those numbers is surprisingly rich.

### Vectors as column matrices

First of all, we can represent vectors as matrices. For example, the vector $(1,1)$ is written as the following column vector:

$$P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Note: In this post, column vectors and coordinate vectors are used interchangeably.

### Standard basis vectors

The familiar $xy$-plane is a 2D space. Its most basic basis vectors are:

$$\mathbf{i} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{j} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Using these column vectors, we can express every vector in 2D space. For instance, $(3,2) = 3\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j}$. Yet other pairs of vectors can also span 2D space. Consider:

$$\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Even though $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are not $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, the previous example $(3,2)$ can still be expressed as $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$.

The vectors $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$ are called the **standard basis vectors**. Other vectors can also represent the entire 2D space, but the standard basis vectors are the reference.

### Matrices as space transformations

Consider what happens if we stretch the usual $xy$-plane by a factor of 2 along the $x$-axis. Every $x$-coordinate doubles: a point that was at $x=4$ moves to $x=8$, and a point at $x=2$ moves to $x=4$. The $y$-coordinates are unchanged. Vectors move with the space, so the transformation can be described by the matrix $A$ below.

The meaning of matrix $A$ is:

- map the standard basis vector $\mathbf{i}=(1,0)$ to $(2,0)$, and  
- map the standard basis vector $\mathbf{j}=(0,1)$ to $(0,1)$.

If a vector before transformation was $3\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j}$, then after stretching the $x$-axis by 2, the same linear combination now represents a vector whose $x$-coordinate is doubled. This process is expressed using matrix multiplication.

Let $\mathbf{X}$ be an arbitrary vector. Then the product $A\mathbf{X}$ means: move $\mathbf{X}$ according to the transformation encoded by $A$, and read off its new position.

The rule for matrix multiplication is: take each row of $A$ and dot it with each column of $\mathbf{X}$. The resulting $(4,1)$ vector is the image of the original $\mathbf{X}=(2,1)$ under the transformation by $A$.

### Where are these transformations used?

One common example is font styling. When slanting or otherwise reshaping text, the program applies a space transformation to every glyph. For instance, applying the “double the $x$-coordinate” transformation to ordinary text yields a horizontally stretched style.

## Matrices and equations

We can also write systems of equations using matrices. Consider the system:

$$
\begin{cases}
ax + by = e \\
cx + dy = f
\end{cases}
$$

This can be written in matrix form as:

$$A\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{B}$$

Since $A$ is the matrix representing a space transformation, this equation asks: find the pre-image $\mathbf{X}$ whose image under the transformation $A$ is the vector $\mathbf{B}$.

A 3-variable system has a similar interpretation:

$$A\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{B}$$

Here, the solution is the vector $\mathbf{X}$ that lands at $\mathbf{B}$ after applying the transformation $A$.

Gaussian elimination is still the simplest method for solving systems, but this geometric viewpoint is worth keeping in mind.
